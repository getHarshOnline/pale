# Help people choose without unnecessary effort

Start with what the person is trying to do, the information needed at that moment and the application state that changes. Reduce simultaneous unrelated decisions and ambiguous signals before removing useful content. A dense view with clear grouping and one clear next step can be easier to use than a sparse view requiring recall, guessing or several concealed steps. Keep related values visible where they are used. Group by a meaningful relationship; reserve strong emphasis for the information or action that deserves attention. A fixed menu length or a remembered cognitive-capacity number is no substitute for observing the task.

Preserve familiar behavior unless a demonstrated task benefit justifies a change. Navigation retains a real address, browser history, modifier activation and a new-context path. Actions use controls whose names explain their effect. Do not turn every result list into a combobox, every tool into a modal, or every site into a command interface. A local deterministic search needs no model. Where an assistant is optional, navigation and essential destinations remain usable without it; unavailable intelligence is an honest state of that tool.

## Choose how much to show

| Problem in the task | Change to try | What to observe |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Choices differ along three dimensions | Group by the dimension the person actually knows, then show the other two where they choose. | Can they find a plausible choice without reading every item? |
| The task makes people recall a value from a previous screen | Keep the value beside the field that uses it. | Can they complete the field without opening or memorizing the earlier page? |
| A control needs precision under time pressure | Enlarge its real target or bring it closer to the current point of interaction. | Is the target easier to hit by touch and pointer? |
| Labels look like one group and behave like two | Change spacing, headings or controls to show the real relationship. | Does grouping predict what clicking does? |
| Progress feels slow | Show an immediate response and the stage the work has reached. | Can someone tell accepted input from completed work? |

Hick’s law depends on how choices are structured and learned. It is a reason to reduce unrelated decisions, not a universal menu count. Memory capacity varies with the material and grouping. Grouping and similarity can make a dense view easier to scan when they reflect the actual task. Fitts’s law concerns distance and usable target size. Increasing the decorative shape while keeping its target small leaves the aiming problem in place. Respect familiar browser and platform behavior until the task gives a reason to change it. Test the complete interaction in context, including the ending. A pleasing finish cannot rescue an unsuccessful task.

A distinctive item can attract attention among similar items (the Von Restorff effect), but a color-only distinction can disappear for some readers. Pair the emphasis with position, wording, shape or another cue that carries the same difference. The peak-end effect is a reason to inspect the difficult moment and the ending of a task, including recovery and confirmation; it does not make the intervening steps or successful completion optional.

## Choose an aid for the kind of attention it needs

At rest, the page carries its whole reading task, a visible position and reachable entry points. A local aid can answer an inquiry beside an element without moving the reading line. A command surface changes attention only after a deliberate action and closes back to the reading context. Links retain their address, history, modifier activation and right-click menu across all three. A shortcut serves people who know it while visible controls serve the rest.

Reduce decisions by grouping comparable choices and making their consequences visible. Keep a remembered value in view when the next step depends on it. Put the label close to its control and make the available action clear through its actual shape and placement. Check the action after one use and after repeated use. A new user may need an explanation of an unfamiliar option, while a frequent user benefits from recognition and a stable route. Smaller visual groups and fewer exposed decisions solve different problems.

## Worked example: keep reference values in an edit

A member editing billing details needs the saved company name and account number. Display both beside the form, then group billing address and tax details under their own headings. Keep the explanatory information people read before deciding. Try the revised form with a new member and an experienced one. If either has to leave the form to find an existing value, put that value beside the relevant field.
