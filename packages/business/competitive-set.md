# Choose which alternatives belong in a comparison

Start with the customer's choice and the decision the analysis must support. That determines which alternatives belong. Use market research when the market itself is unclear, then compare the evidence after choosing the set.

## Define the customer's choice

State the decision: positioning, product scope, market entry, pricing, partnership, acquisition or response to a rival. Name who decides and what evidence could change the action. Define the customer task or outcome; buyer, user and payer; geography, period and channel; purchase occasion and budget source; and any regulatory or technical eligibility conditions.

Ask what the customer could choose in that situation. Consider:

- another offer purchased for substantially the same task;
- a different category that satisfies enough of the task to redirect demand or budget;
- doing the work internally or manually, delaying it or doing nothing;
- a supplier, platform or channel that could enter this market within the decision period.

These describe different relationships, without ranking them. An offer may be a close substitute for one segment and irrelevant to another. Shared ownership, technology or appearance is a lead to investigate, not enough to establish competition.

Explain how the alternatives constrain one another: price, quality, fit with the customer's workflow, scarce inputs, distribution, switching costs, bundling or another way to achieve the same outcome. A benchmark may illustrate a capability or standard even when the customer cannot buy it. Include it as a benchmark when that informs the decision, with its different role clear.

## Test each candidate's place in the set

For every candidate, explain why it might change the choice. Look for switching, overlapping bids or consideration lists, shared budgets, common distribution, comparable use, reasons for lost sales, market entry or capacity changes in response to demand. Give each candidate one state:

- **Included:** evidence supports its relevance to this decision.
- **Watch:** the relationship could matter, but its evidence or timing remains unclear.
- **Excluded:** it fails a stated condition or cannot affect the decision period.

Keep the reason for an exclusion in the comparison when someone could reasonably contest it. Lists from management, searches, vendors and market reports suggest candidates; check their relevance yourself.

Separate present competition from possible future competition. A firm lacking distribution, certification, capacity or a compatible offer may belong on the watch list if those limits could change in time. A prominent brand outside the buyer's available choices earns no place merely through attention.

Challenge the set in both directions. Weak, obsolete or distant alternatives can flatter the offer; unavailable alternatives can make it look artificially poor. Keep asymmetry visible: one offer can constrain another without the reverse mattering equally, especially across different sizes, channels and bundles.

## Keep the segment and time visible

Use different sets where choices change by segment, occasion or geography. Give the comparison's as-of date and time horizon. Entry, exit, product retirement, ownership, regulation, channel access and pricing can all change the set while the method stays useful.

Use the smallest set that can test the decision. Add a candidate when it offers a different route to the outcome, challenges an assumption or could change the recommended action. More names alone add little evidence and can suggest comparisons that are not valid.

## Return a set another reader can check

In the comparison, give each candidate's identity, proposed relationship, segment and horizon, inclusion state, supporting and opposing evidence, confidence, need for current information and reason it could change the decision. Say where you searched and which blind spots remain.

Finish when each material way of meeting the need is represented, contested exclusions have reasons, and another candidate is unlikely to change the comparison. Pass that set to the evidence-comparison stage. Scores, attribute claims and a winner need comparable evidence before they can be added.

Rapid entry, opaque buyer behavior, segment differences or missing switching evidence may leave several plausible sets. Return those alternatives and show whether the decision changes between them. Leave the uncertainty visible even if the next stage expects one table.

## Worked example

A small team deciding whether to buy scheduling software used a shared calendar in its last two projects. Include the calendar as an alternative for that segment. A large vendor's tool is available only through a channel those teams cannot use this year. Keep it as a benchmark if a feature informs the product decision. Watch its channel launch if that would change what buyers can choose.
