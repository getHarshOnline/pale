# Compare the alternatives a customer can choose

Start with the customer's choice and the decision the analysis must support. That determines which alternatives belong. Use market research when the market itself is unclear, then compare the evidence after choosing the set. Use current public evidence supplied through the task’s permitted research tools.

## Define the customer's choice

State the decision: positioning, product scope, market entry, pricing, partnership, acquisition or response to a rival. Name who decides and what evidence could change the action. Define the customer task or outcome; buyer, user and payer; geography, period and channel; purchase occasion and budget source; and any regulatory or technical eligibility conditions.

Ask what the customer could choose in that situation. Consider:

- another offer purchased for substantially the same task;
- a different category that satisfies enough of the task to redirect demand or budget;
- doing the work internally or manually, delaying it or doing nothing;
- a supplier, platform or channel that could enter this market within the decision period.

These describe different relationships, without ranking them. An offer may be a close substitute for one segment and irrelevant to another. Shared ownership, technology or appearance is a lead to investigate, not enough to establish competition.

Explain how the alternatives constrain one another: price, quality, fit with the customer's workflow, scarce inputs, distribution, switching costs, bundling or another way to achieve the same outcome. A benchmark may illustrate a capability or standard even when the customer cannot buy it. Include it as a benchmark when that informs the decision, with its different role clear.

## Test each candidate's place in the set

For every candidate, explain why it might change the choice. Look for switching, overlapping bids or consideration lists, shared budgets, common distribution, comparable use, reasons for lost sales, market entry or capacity changes in response to demand. Give each candidate one state:

- **Included:** evidence supports its relevance to this decision.
- **Watch:** the relationship could matter, but its evidence or timing remains unclear.
- **Excluded:** it fails a stated condition or cannot affect the decision period.

Keep the reason for an exclusion in the comparison when someone could reasonably contest it. Lists from management, searches, vendors and market reports suggest candidates; check their relevance yourself.

Separate present competition from possible future competition. A firm lacking distribution, certification, capacity or a compatible offer may belong on the watch list if those limits could change in time. A prominent brand outside the buyer's available choices earns no place merely through attention.

Challenge the set in both directions. Weak, obsolete or distant alternatives can flatter the offer; unavailable alternatives can make it look artificially poor. Keep asymmetry visible: one offer can constrain another without the reverse mattering equally, especially across different sizes, channels and bundles.

## Keep the segment and time visible

Use different sets where choices change by segment, occasion or geography. Give the comparison's as-of date and time horizon. Entry, exit, product retirement, ownership, regulation, channel access and pricing can all change the set while the method stays useful.

Use the smallest set that can test the decision. Add a candidate when it offers a different route to the outcome, challenges an assumption or could change the recommended action. More names alone add little evidence and can suggest comparisons that are not valid.

## Return a set another reader can check

In the comparison, give each candidate's identity, proposed relationship, segment and horizon, inclusion state, supporting and opposing evidence, confidence, need for current information and reason it could change the decision. Say where you searched and which blind spots remain.

Finish when each material way of meeting the need is represented, contested exclusions have reasons, and another candidate is unlikely to change the comparison. Pass that set to the evidence-comparison stage. Scores, attribute claims and a winner need comparable evidence before they can be added.

Rapid entry, opaque buyer behavior, segment differences or missing switching evidence may leave several plausible sets. Return those alternatives and show whether the decision changes between them. Leave the uncertainty visible even if the next stage expects one table.

## Worked example

A small team deciding whether to buy scheduling software used a shared calendar in its last two projects. Include the calendar as an alternative for that segment. A large vendor's tool is available only through a channel those teams cannot use this year. Keep it as a benchmark if a feature informs the product decision. Watch its channel launch if that would change what buyers can choose.

## Define each comparison before collecting claims

Identify the decision and the hypotheses it depends on. Choose dimensions where a difference could change the action: eligibility, fit with the customer's work, total cost, switching cost, channel access, service limits or a credible direction of development. Easy-to-find data alone is a poor reason to add a dimension.

For each dimension, define its meaning, unit or observable indicator, customer and use case, period, and what would count against the claim. If the same definition does not fit every alternative, split the dimension or show the mismatch.

## Keep each claim small enough to check

In the comparison's evidence notes, give each claim's subject, attribute, scope, as-of date, source, status and confidence. Use these statuses:

- **Reported:** asserted by the company or another interested party.
- **Observed:** directly visible in a product, filing, price, behavior or repeatable check.
- **Corroborated:** supported by sufficiently independent evidence.
- **Inferred:** concluded from stated premises.
- **Unresolved:** material evidence is missing or conflicting.

A company's claim can be relevant without being independent confirmation. Articles repeating one announcement share a source. Leave absent evidence as unknown; an undocumented competitor should not receive a worse substantive judgment merely because another publishes more.

## Put the claims on the same basis

Carry the same comparison unit from the candidate list into the evidence for each claim. An analogous service in another channel may illustrate an idea while leaving open whether customers can substitute it today or whether it is deployed. When amounts, units, timing or conditions conflict, keep the mismatch visible and name the source or observation needed to resolve it.

Align currency, tax treatment, contract duration, product version, geography, customer size, included services, usage limits and period. Distinguish list price from actual cost, feature availability from usability, announced from shipped capabilities, and one observation from persistent behavior. Show where comparisons would require unsupported assumptions.

Seek evidence against the emerging explanation. For each material dimension, show agreement, conflict and absence. When sources disagree, examine time, definitions, segment, method and access before deciding whether uncertainty remains. Keep a conflict visible until a source or observation settles it.

## Explain judgment without inventing precision

Qualitative labels can make a review consistent. Define the labels, show their supporting claims and state confidence. Adding or averaging ordered labels, or weighting them as if measured, needs a defensible scale and aggregation model. A decisive constraint can outweigh many favourable observations without an artificial number.

For each hypothesis, explain what the evidence supports, what it weakens, what remains unknown and what that means for the action. A rival's advantage might call for avoidance, differentiation, partnership, faster testing or no response.

Try scenarios that could change the recommendation: a watched candidate enters, a channel closes, a reported feature fails its check, a price cannot be compared, or the buyer values a different constraint. Tie each claimed threat, opportunity or trade-off to evidence relevant to the decision. For every recommended action, name its trigger and what observation would advance or reverse it.

### Negative example

A polished report compares twelve companies in a colour-coded matrix. It fills blanks with the lowest score, treats vendor pages as independent confirmation, mixes current enterprise prices with retired self-service prices and averages the labels into one winner. The set is untested, missing evidence became a penalty, definitions differ and the ranking cannot be traced to comparable evidence. Revisit the chosen alternatives and claims before improving the chart.

## Return a comparison the decision-maker can use

Return the chosen set, definitions, individual claims and their evidence, comparable findings, contradictions, unresolved fields, information that may be stale, and a recommendation tied to what would change the decision. Name the smallest observation that would resolve each critical uncertainty.

Finish when every critical hypothesis is supported, contradicted or explicitly unresolved, further collection is unlikely to change the action, and the recommendation makes sense without a decorative score. Keep dated competitor facts with the owner's reviewed dataset or replayable source, including a Pack where appropriate. The reusable Package holds the method. This analysis does not contact competitors, buy access, publish claims or change products or market records.

## Before handing off

- [ ] Do the alternatives and comparison definitions fit this customer’s decision, segment and time horizon?
- [ ] Can each claim be traced to its evidence, with reported, observed and inferred findings distinguished?
- [ ] Are missing evidence, conflicting terms and comparisons that cannot be made still visible?
- [ ] Does the recommendation explain what would change it and the smallest observation needed for each critical unknown?
