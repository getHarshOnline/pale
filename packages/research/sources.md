# Find the evidence a question still needs

Read the subject’s existing research, explanation and linked sources before choosing a search. Follow related work when it can change the question or answer. Search in successive rounds when the needed evidence remains missing. Retrieval finds possible evidence; the research guide judges what it supports.

Start with the claim, the evidence needed to decide it and the smallest authoritative source likely to answer it. Choose the search location, terms or identifiers and time range for that gap. After each round, distinguish findings, contradictions, absence, inaccessible material and stale results. Keep exact strategies, dates and exclusions when a study’s coverage or reproducibility depends on them; ordinary retrieval does not require a diary of each query.

Let the remaining gap determine the next query. Change source type, definition, period or method deliberately. Similar queries over one source history add no independence. Summaries and indexes help navigation; inspect the original source before relying on a consequential claim.

Explain the coverage, access limits and gaps that affect the finding, including a failed route when it could conceal decisive evidence. Finish when the decision has enough support, a named external observation is needed or further retrieval cannot change the result. Put useful findings and their sources into the existing research account through the evidence guide; an unchanged conclusion needs no new search log or handoff document.

If a route is unavailable, retain the query or source needed, claims left open and any supplied snapshot supporting a narrower dated finding. Search repositories or local indexes within the permitted scope. Use a current provider only when connected and appropriate. Keep private names, excerpts, secrets and unpublished assumptions out of external queries and connectors.

## Follow an index to the saved source

Read the identified item before citing it. Give the reader a usable reference to the original source and the revision or observation date when it changes the claim. For a saved item, distinguish original bytes, an extract and an analyst’s summary; include its saved identity and location when needed to retrieve or verify that evidence. An extract’s digest identifies the extract; mark claims that need the original. Source traceability belongs with the finding, not in a separate ledger for every handoff.

When a location moves, verify the saved identity before updating the link. When content changes, identify a separate revision and compare the relevant evidence. Preserve dated observations and connect corrections to affected claims. Name an unresolved saved item as missing. An authorised current lookup can add a new observation, but cannot reconstruct the earlier capture.

## Before handing off

- [ ] Did each search address a material unresolved question?
- [ ] Were original sources read, with their versions and saved identities distinguished from extracts or summaries?
- [ ] Are useful findings with their owning research, and coverage or access limits that could change the answer still visible?
